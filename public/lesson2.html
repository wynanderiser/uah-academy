Beginner tier · Lesson 2: Two Objects, One Light

Two objects, sharing one light.

Before you begin

Today we bring a second object into the picture. Nothing about drawing one thing well prepares you for two, because the moment a second object arrives, everything becomes a comparison. Is the mug really that much bigger than the egg beside it, or does it only look that way from where you're sitting? Does the light fall the same way across both, or does the glossy cup argue with the matt fruit next to it? A single object only has to be itself. Two objects have to agree — on the same light, the same distance, the same page. This lesson asks you to hold both at once.

Getting the size relationship between two objects honest, and keeping the same light logic working across both. That's it — two things, but harder than they sound.

What you'll need
  • Two simple objects, genuinely different in size or shape — not two eggs, but something like a mug and an egg, or an apple and a pear of noticeably different sizes
  • Whatever's nearest to hand to draw with — the same as Lesson 1
  • The same single light source from one side — consistency with what you learned in Lesson 1 matters here more than ever
The exercise (15–20 minutes)
  1. Place them close. Put the two objects near each other, slightly overlapping if that feels natural — you want to actually compare them, not draw two separate little scenes.
  2. Compare before you commit. Hold your pencil up and roughly measure how many times the smaller object fits into the larger one, before you commit either outline to the page.
  3. Grid it lightly first. Draw a simple 3×3 grid very lightly in pencil across your page before you start — three columns, three rows. It gives you honest reference points for where each object should sit and how big it should be, rather than guessing freehand.

    The grid is a tool, not a rule — here are two completely different, equally valid arrangements using the same grid.

    Both objects low and central — the mug filling roughly two cells, the egg about one.

    The same two objects, arranged completely differently — mug high and centred, egg tucked lower right.

  4. One light, both objects. Apply the same shading logic from Lesson 1 to both forms consistently — if the light comes from the left, both objects need their dark side on the right.

That's the whole brief, same as before. Getting the proportion between the two roughly honest matters more than getting either one perfect alone.
