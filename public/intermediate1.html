Intermediate tier · Lesson 1: Perspective Basics

Show me something that exists in real space.

Before you begin

Everything so far has been about a single, fixed view — objects the size they appear, sitting exactly where you placed them, no real sense of a world extending beyond the page. Perspective is the tool that changes that. Look down any street and the parallel edges of the pavement seem to lean toward each other in the distance, meeting at a point that doesn't actually exist anywhere except in how your eye reads depth. Once you know where that point is, and put it in the right place, anything you draw starts to feel like it exists somewhere, rather than floating on paper. This lesson covers that mechanic — and then a second thing that follows straight on from it: where you actually put the horizon is itself a real decision, not just a default.

Learning to use a single vanishing point and horizon line so objects genuinely recede into distance — and understanding that where you put that horizon is itself a compositional choice, not just a default. This is properly new territory — take your time with it.

What you'll need
  • A simple box-like object — a book, a tissue box, a small parcel — or a real view down a corridor or street, something with genuine straight receding edges to observe
  • A ruler this time — accurate straight lines matter here in a way they haven't before
  • A harder pencil this time — an HB, or even an H
A note on this pencil switch

Lesson 3 asked for something soft, because the goal there was real, expressive pressure variation. This lesson asks for the opposite. A perspective sketch is a plan, not a finished piece — you're testing where things should go, which means redrawing lines more than once. A harder pencil keeps construction lines light, precise, and easy to correct, rather than committing too early to marks you'll want to erase. The tool follows the purpose, not a fixed habit.

Part 1 — The vanishing point (15–20 minutes)
  1. Find your horizon line. Draw a single horizontal line across your page at your own eye level — this represents where the horizon would be if you could see it.
  2. Place one vanishing point. Choose a single point on that horizon line. Every receding edge in your drawing will aim toward this one point.

    The dashed lines are your construction guides — every receding edge of the box aims at the same single point.

  3. Draw toward the point. For any edge that recedes away from you — the top and bottom of a box's side, for instance — draw it as a straight line heading toward your vanishing point, not parallel to the page edge.
Part 2 — Where you put the horizon (15–20 minutes)
  1. Try the centre first. Your horizon so far has probably sat somewhere in the middle. That's a completely valid choice — calm, symmetrical — and worth noticing on purpose rather than by accident.

    Same box, same vanishing point logic — only the horizon moves. Notice how much the balance of ground and sky changes the feel.

    Centre — calm, symmetrical

    Upper third — ground dominates, grounded and close

    Lower third — sky dominates, open and expansive

  2. Now move it. On a fresh attempt, redraw the same scene with the horizon in the upper third, then the lower third if you have room — same vanishing point logic, just relocated.
  3. Notice what changes. Which version feels most like the scene you actually intended? There's no wrong answer here — but there should be an answer, a deliberate one, not an accident of where you happened to draw the first line.

That's the whole brief. Get comfortable with one vanishing point before ever worrying about two — and remember that even a "correct" perspective drawing still has a real choice sitting inside it: where the horizon goes.
