Intermediate tier · Lesson 3: Composition in Depth

Directing the eye, not just filling the page.

Before you begin

A drawing doesn't show a viewer everything at once — their eye has to land somewhere first, then travel somewhere next. Left entirely to chance, an eye tends to wander toward the middle of a page and just stop there, which is exactly why so many first attempts at composition end up with the subject stranded dead-centre, technically visible but doing nothing. Painters have known for centuries that a couple of deliberate choices change this completely: place the important thing slightly off-centre, and it gains a kind of tension and life a centred subject never quite has. Give the eye a path — a fence line, a shoreline, a fold of shadow — and it will follow that path toward whatever waits at the end of it. This lesson is about making those two choices on purpose, rather than leaving them to chance.

Placing your subject deliberately off-centre using the rule of thirds, and using a real or implied line within the scene to lead the eye toward it. Two tools working together, for directing attention rather than hoping it lands somewhere useful.

What you'll need
  • A subject with a real line somewhere in the scene — a table edge, a shadow, a fold of fabric, anything that could plausibly lead the eye toward your subject
  • The same materials as before — a soft pencil for the drawing itself, a ruler if your leading line is straight
  • The same single light source from one side
The exercise (35–50 minutes)
  1. Grid it, thirds this time. Use the same 3×3 grid technique from Lesson 1 — but this time, place your subject's most important point where two grid lines actually cross, not in the centre square.

    Same grid, two very different results — dead-centre versus a thirds intersection, with a line leading the eye there.

    Dead-centre — technically visible, doing nothing

    On a thirds intersection, with a line leading there

  2. Find or invent a leading line. Look for a real line in your scene — an edge, a shadow, a receding surface — that naturally points toward your subject. If nothing obvious presents itself, you can choose to add one deliberately, the same way you'd choose where to place a shadow.
  3. Check the whole page once more. Does the eye now have somewhere to land, and a way of getting there? If your subject is still just floating in the middle, thirds and leading lines are being described but not actually used.

That's the whole brief. Everything from earlier lessons still matters here — this adds two deliberate tools on top, it doesn't replace the fundamentals.
